## TL;DR
- **The evidence does not support a characteristic standoff distance.** Defenders at the Little Bighorn established themselves on TERRAIN FEATURES — a low hill/"bench" and foothills on Reno's open left/west flank, the timber and brush along the river, and dry channels/coulees on the valley floor — not at a fixed range from the lodges. The apparent distances (~90–550 m) are incidental to where cover and enfilading ground happened to lie.
- **The two independent cases agree.** At Ford B / Medicine Tail (Custer's approach), defenders again used terrain — the west (village-side) riverbank, cutbank, brush, and a quick crossing to the east bank — not a measured line. Four comparative Plains engagements (Powder River, Slim Buttes, Dull Knife, Wolf Mountain) show the same pattern: defenders take bluffs, ravines, and gullies.
- **Verdict = TERRAIN; §E standoff range = null.** The warrior response was fast, reactive, and terrain-exploiting rather than a deliberate screening line at a set distance. There is weak, indirect support that the defence bought time for a non-combatant flight north/northwest, but no source frames it as a range-holding screen.

## Key Findings
1. Reno halted and dismounted roughly **90–550 m** short of the Hunkpapa lodges depending on source and datum — a spread reflecting the cavalry's stopping point, NOT a warrior standoff line.
2. Warriors did not form a fixed line at a set distance; they **boiled out of the lodges**, initially circled/skirmished mounted at long range (per Lt. Varnum's RCOI testimony, warriors held ranges of "800, some 1000, and some took 1200 yard range" and their fire fell short), then rapidly closed and worked onto the **low hill/bench and foothills on Reno's exposed left (west)**, turning his flank and driving him into the timber. Scout Billy Jackson's account (via Wikipedia) records "an attack of more than 500 warriors against the left and rear of Reno's line."
3. The physical archaeology of the valley floor is largely on **private land (Jason Pitsch's survey)** and is not fully published, so the warrior firing positions in §A rest chiefly on Native testimony and cavalry testimony, not on published cartridge-case mapping.
4. Ford B fighting was terrain-anchored on the **riverbank/cutbank/brush**; NPS interpretation (citing archaeological evidence) supports light, brief action there.
5. Comparative engagements uniformly show terrain occupation (bluffs, ravines, gullies), reinforcing a TERRAIN reading — but most are dawn surprise attacks and should not be used to bound the Little Bighorn daylight case.

**Caveats (§A):** The warrior firing positions are known primarily from **Native testimony (via Michno/Hardorff) and cavalry testimony (RCOI)**, NOT from published valley-floor archaeology. Douglas Scott/Richard Fox's systematic cartridge-case mapping concentrated on the Custer field and Reno–Benteen defense site; the valley skirmish-line ground was surveyed privately by Jason Pitsch on private land and is not fully published. Moylan's RCOI figure "not over three hundred yards distant there was quite a number of Indian lodges in sight" is measured from the timber where the horses were held (the right/rear), not from the skirmish-line front. Varnum's testimony that warrior fire from the foothills at 800–1,200 yards "fell short" is direct evidence that the initial defence was mobile long-range skirmishing from terrain, not a held line at a lethal standoff range. Distance figures therefore describe where the cavalry stopped, not where the defenders stood.

**DIRECT VERDICT: TERRAIN.** The record describes the defenders **occupying specific terrain features** — the timber/brush along the river, the low hill/"Bench" and foothills on Reno's open left, and the riverbank/cutbank at Ford B — and any apparent distance is **incidental to where the cover and flanking ground happened to be.** Three lines of evidence force this reading: (1) the defenders did not hold a line at a range — they closed aggressively from mounted long-range skirmishing (Varnum: fire from 800–1,200 yards fell short) to point-blank, overrunning Reno's flank within ~15 minutes; (2) the "distances" in the record (90–550 m) are measurements of where the *cavalry* stopped or of village width (Moylan's 200–300 yards is village frontage, per Michno p.18), not of a warrior standoff position; (3) the second, independent case (Ford B) and all four comparative engagements show the same feature-occupation behaviour with no consistent range. A residual "phased" argument exists (initial mounted circling at long range, then movement onto features) but the initial phase was mobile skirmishing, not a deliberate screening line, so BOTH_PHASED overstates the evidence. **§E returns null.**

**Caveats (§F):** Washita, Powder River, Slim Buttes, and Dull Knife were all **surprise dawn attacks on sleeping/unalerted camps** — a fundamentally different tactical problem from the Little Bighorn's **daylight approach against an alerted village**. They should NOT be used to numerically bound the Little Bighorn standoff; they are cited only to corroborate the qualitative pattern that defenders occupy **terrain (ravines, gullies, bluffs)** rather than standing off at a set distance. Wolf Mountain is a role-reversal (Indians attacking a soldier camp) and is weakest for the analogy. The Dull Knife lodge count is disputed (173 vs 200) — reported as a spread, not averaged.

## §H. NEGATIVE FINDINGS

- **Published valley-floor archaeology of the warrior firing positions is the critical missing evidence.** Scott/Fox's systematic cartridge-case mapping (SCOTT1987; FOX1993) covered the Custer field and Reno–Benteen defense site. The Reno *valley* skirmish-line ground and the adjacent camp areas were metal-detected by **Jason Pitsch on private land**; those results are referenced (e.g., ACWRT summary) but a full published cartridge-distribution map placing warriors relative to the skirmish line could not be located. This is where HIGH-confidence physical evidence would come from, and it is not accessible here.
- **No author-specific yardage** for skirmish-line-to-lodge distance with an explicit datum (edge vs. centre) could be confirmed verbatim from Michno (1997) or Gray (1991). The widely repeated "200–300 yards" is village *width* (Moylan/Michno p.18), not standoff distance — a figure that should NOT be transcribed as a standoff range.
- **Gray's exact minute-level times** could not be confirmed from the book itself (page cites not located); they reach us via a secondary reconstruction, and Gray's sun-time-to-standard-clock offset is unconfirmed. Times in §C are therefore MEDIUM at best.
- **Coordinates for the valley fight are uncertain** (±200–400 m). The NPS auto-tour markers are interpretive placements on the bluff tour road, not the actual valley locations, and the actual ground is on private land near Garryowen.
- **Figures traceable only to tier-5 reminiscence and not to be trusted as standoff data:** popular "within ¼ mile of the Indian camp" (Garryowen roadside marker), and round-number "500 yards" claims in general histories. These are consistent with the terrain reading but carry no standoff-distance authority.
- **Sources that would settle the question but are not accessible:** the full Pitsch valley survey data (private land / unpublished); Hardorff's *Lakota Recollections* / *Cheyenne Memories* primary compilations (referenced but not read in full here); the complete Michno *Lakota Noon* time-segment maps (pp. xiii–end); Gray (1991) chronology pages. These are held in the published books and in private collections/archives rather than online.
- **Dull Knife lodge count disputed** (173 WyoHistory vs 200 Wikipedia) — flagged, not reconciled.
