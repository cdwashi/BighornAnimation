# O8-MELEE — Close-Action Lethality in Plains Warfare Against US Cavalry, 1860s–1877

## Scope note
This report answers one question: how do men kill at contact in Plains warfare against US cavalry, 1860s–1877? As the prompt predicted, the strongest evidence class is the archaeology and osteology of the Little Bighorn dead (Q3); it is treated as the anchor. The rate question (Q1) comes back weak, and I say so plainly. The native shape of the evidence (Q2) is the resolution of a closing event — an overrun — not a rate over time. Per the circularity guard, Little Bighorn battle-aggregate casualty totals are excluded as a derivation base; per-mechanism (trauma typology), per-incident (named episodes with local counts), and comparative-engagement evidence (Fetterman especially) are used.

## Q1 — Casualty production as a rate: CONFIRMED-WEAK

The scholarly and primary literature does not support a casualties-per-man-per-minute rate for hand-to-hand contact in this theater, and I record that as the finding, not as a shortfall in searching. The reason is structural. Every well-documented close-action episode is described as the resolution of a closing event — an overrun — whose duration is given qualitatively ("within minutes," "like a buffalo hunt," "the whole thing took less than two hours") rather than clocked against a body count. Where hard numbers exist, they attach to episodes, not intervals: "more than 30" troopers killed in Reno's retreat rout (NPS marker), 28 bodies counted in Deep Ravine (Sgt. Daniel Kanipe). Converting either into a rate requires dividing by an assumed time-in-contact — precisely the circular derivation the prompt forbids, because that same battle's aggregate casualties are the simulation's grading key. The honest verdict is that Q1 is weak as a rate; the useful answer lives in Q2 and Q3.

The two kill directions are not merged. Warrior-on-trooper close action was lethal and repeated (the dominant killing direction once cohesion broke). Trooper-on-warrior close action was rare and low-yield: across the comparative engagements (Wolf Mountain, Powder River/Reynolds, Slim Buttes), warrior deaths attributable to soldiers closing to contact are in the low single digits per action, and most of those occurred in village surprise attacks or against a fixed ravine position (Slim Buttes' American Horse), not in cavalry-initiated melee.

## Q2 — Native shape: the resolution of a closing event

The evidence is unambiguously event-shaped. Richard Fox's archaeological reconstruction of the Custer field is explicit: the five companies "entered the fray in good order … displaying tactical stability," and it was "the sudden disintegration of this cohesion that caused the troopers' defeat. The end came quickly, unexpectedly, and largely amid terror and disarray." Fox found "no determined fighting and little firearm resistance"; the last soldiers "had rushed from Custer Hill" and were run down. The correct modelling primitive is therefore a **closing-event resolver with an outcome distribution** — the contacted fragment is (a) annihilated in place (Deep Ravine; Keogh's company, which Lt. John Bourke observed "died in one compact mass"), (b) put to flight (Reno's rout across the river), or (c) able to repulse the rush (Reno's initial skirmish line held ~20 minutes before collapse; Crazy Horse's mounted rushes at Wolf Mountain were repulsed by Miles's perimeter). Forcing a per-minute attrition rate onto this record would misrepresent the sources' own shape.

Warrior testimony reinforces the event shape and the outcome distribution. American Horse (Cheyenne) on the retreat crossing: "The Indians rode right up to them and knocked some off their horses as they were running … It was like chasing buffalo … a grand chase." Iron Hawk (Hunkpapa) on the Company E men fleeing to Deep Ravine: "Nearly all of them were afoot … so scared they didn't know what they were doing." Respects Nothing (Lakota): "The soldiers at Custer Hill were all killed before those down along the ravine." The uniform picture is momentary contact that resolves a fragment's fate in a single short bout.

## Q3 — The archaeological proportion (the anchor)

This is the strongest and most decision-relevant evidence, and it points to a two-stage mechanism: **gunfire wounds and disables; close action finishes.** Clyde Snow's published report on the 1984–85 excavations stated that the osteological evidence "supports a scenario of the events as consisting of a brief firefight followed by the close-range dispatch of the wounded … It appears probable that the majority of the troopers were still alive but more or less helplessly wounded when resistance ceased and that many were finished off with massive crushing blows to the head." Snow and Fitzpatrick analyzed the remains of more than 20 individuals from the excavations (the exact integer and the per-category tallies live in their Table 27, p. 252, and Table 28, pp. 274–75, which could not be read verbatim — see Access Register).

The quantitative trauma proportions from the Custer-field archaeological record (Douglas Scott):
- **Blunt-instrument cranial trauma: all 14 skull-bearing cases (41% of Custer-field individuals).** Scott: "There are 14 cases in the Custer battlefield archeological record in which skull fragments were present, and all exhibit blunt instrument trauma. This group accounts for 41 percent of the Custer battlefield individuals represented archeologically."
- **Incised (cut) wounds: ~21%** of Custer-field remains — versus a single case at the Reno-Benteen defense site.
- **Knife- or arrow-related wounds: 11%; hatchet-related: 10%.**
- In the separate, gunshot-focused sample of Willey & Scott (1996), **6 of 10 skeletons had gunshot wounds** (3 cranial vault, 1 innominate, 1 humerus, 1 chest region). This sample and the mutilation counts are different populations and must not be summed.
- Mielke's (2018) review of 31 individuals found **only 3 self-inflicted head gunshots but 22 with mutilation-consistent damage**, with the explicit caveat "No doubt suicides happened among Custer's men, but perhaps not on the grand scale previously suggested."

**Stated confidence and the governing bias.** The analysts are explicit about limits. Scott: the extent of mutilation "cannot be precisely determined because of the lack of tissue and because many of the remains are missing some skeletal elements." Three biases must be carried into any simulation use:
1. **Recovery bias.** The sample is fragmentary and non-random: ~210 men died on the Custer field, only ~44 individuals have ever been recovered since 1877, and the 28 Deep Ravine bodies have never been found. The recovered set over-represents locations that were excavated and under-represents whole categories of death.
2. **Taphonomy.** Bones are heavily eroded, sometimes charred, and commingled, so many injuries that affected only soft tissue leave no trace and are structurally invisible.
3. **Perimortem-vs-postmortem ambiguity — the single most important caveat.** Bone cannot resolve whether blunt-force or edged trauma occurred just before or just after death. The 41% blunt-force-skull figure therefore conflates *killing blows* with *postmortem mutilation of the already-dead*. Snow's interpretation ("dispatch of the wounded") reads much of it as cause-of-death, and the historical mutilation accounts (Sioux/Cheyenne cultural practice) are consistent with a large postmortem component. The osteology alone cannot separate the two. **The archaeology strongly establishes that close-action trauma was pervasive; it does not, by itself, prove what share of deaths was *caused* by close action rather than gunfire.**

**Comparative anchor — Fetterman (1866).** Not archaeologically excavated, but the contemporary record is exceptionally clear and points the same way. Of 81 dead, post surgeon Samuel M. Horton "concluded that no more than six soldiers died from gunshot wounds" (two of those possibly self-inflicted); the remainder died by arrow, lance, club, and knife. Fetterman's own death was recorded by Horton — his throat and neck "cut to the cervical spine all around" and thorax "cut crosswise with a knife, deep into the viscera" — matching the Oglala account crediting American Horse with killing him by knife. Roughly 40,000 arrows were reportedly loosed in an action lasting about 40 minutes. Because the warriors here had few firearms, Fetterman is the cleanest per-incident demonstration that when warriors close, edged/blunt/arrow trauma dominates the death tally.

## Recommendations

1. **Model close action as a closing-event resolver, not a rate (stage 1, immediate).** Give hand-to-hand contact an outcome distribution over {annihilation of the contacted fragment, its flight, its repulse of the rush}, triggered when a contacted unit's cohesion/morale crosses a break threshold. This matches Fox's disintegration model and the event shape of every documented episode, and it avoids fabricating a per-minute rate the sources do not support.

2. **Parameterize a two-stage finishing mechanism (stage 2).** Encode: (a) a fire phase that produces wounded/suppressed men; (b) a cohesion-break trigger; (c) a rush that resolves the fragment's fate, with **close-range dispatch of the disabled as the dominant death producer** once the break occurs. Anchor the "finishing" dominance on Snow's qualitative scenario, not on a manufactured number.

3. **Use the trauma proportions as flagged priors, never as cause-of-death certainties (stage 2).** If the model must split death causes, seed it with blunt-force-cranial ≈41% (all skull cases), incised ≈21%, and gunshot majority-in-focused-sample, but tag every one of these as *perimortem-ambiguous* and require the grading harness to treat them as trauma-typology priors with wide uncertainty. Keep the Willey & Scott gunshot sample and the mutilation counts as separate populations.

4. **Benchmarks/thresholds that would change these rulings:**
   - Publication (or direct reading) of Snow & Fitzpatrick's Table 28 per-category counts, or the Mielke thesis per-individual tables → replace qualitative shares with numeric ones and tighten the Q3 ranges.
   - Any excavation or positive detection of the Deep Ravine 28 → materially revises the recovery-bias picture and the flight-vs-annihilation outcome weights.
   - A peer-reviewed re-analysis that explicitly separates perimortem killing trauma from postmortem mutilation → would convert the 41% blunt-force figure from a trauma-prevalence statistic into a defensible cause-of-death share, and could justify raising the modeled close-action share of *deaths*.

## Caveats

- **Circularity:** No figure used here is derived from Little Bighorn battle-aggregate casualty totals divided by time or contact count. The one such candidate is recorded EXCLUDED-CIRCULAR.
- **Sample fragility:** The osteological samples are small (10 in Willey & Scott; >20 in Snow & Fitzpatrick; 31 in Mielke; ~44 individuals ever recovered) and non-random; recovery and taphonomic biases are severe.
- **Perimortem/postmortem conflation** is the governing epistemic limit on Q3 and is flagged throughout; the trauma-typology proportions are not clean cause-of-death proportions.
- **Testimony conflict, reported not averaged:** Warrior accounts emphasize rapid overrun and "buffalo-chase" dispatch; some older cavalry-derived narratives emphasized organized last-stand resistance and mass suicide. The archaeology (Fox, Snow, Mielke) sides decisively with overrun and close-range dispatch over both organized last-stand resistance and mass suicide. The spread is preserved rather than reconciled to a single number.
- **Comparative engagements** (Fetterman aside) are village-attack or defensive-perimeter actions, not symmetrical cavalry-vs-warrior melee, and are used only for the direction and order of magnitude of close-action outcomes.
